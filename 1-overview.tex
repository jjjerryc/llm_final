\section{Project Overview}
\label{section:intro}
\subsection{Profile}
\paragraph{Track: Application.}
This project studies evidence selection for a large language model (LLM) used to classify factual hallucinations in scientific peer reviews. The study focuses on a controlled comparison of evidence selection strategies under a fixed context budget using the five annotated categories: Attribution Failure, Entity, Number, Overgeneralization, and Temporal.

\paragraph{Abstract and project goal.}
Scientific peer reviews may contain statements about a paper's methods, results, entities, or numerical findings that are unsupported by the paper itself. Detecting the type of such errors requires the model to connect a review statement with relevant evidence from a potentially long scientific document. We investigate whether selecting targeted evidence improves five-class hallucination classification compared with using the review alone or truncating the paper. Using 5,695 training examples and 1,777 labeled development examples with linked source-paper PDFs, we propose a fixed-classifier comparison of review-only input, leading-document truncation, and BM25-based evidence retrieval. We will evaluate macro-averaged F1, per-class performance, resource usage, and representative errors under a controlled context budget. The evaluation uses consistent classification settings and examines how evidence selection affects overall and class-specific performance.

\paragraph{Research Objective.}
This project investigates whether targeted evidence retrieval using BM25 improves LLM-based hallucination-type classification in scientific peer reviews. We compare review-only input, leading-document context, and retrieved evidence under a controlled context budget, using macro F1 and per-class metrics.

\subsection{Motivation}
A scientific review can confidently cite nonexistent experimental settings, incorrect numerical values, or inaccurate statements about a manuscript. A review-only classifier may rely on linguistic cues rather than checking the underlying paper. Conversely, simply inserting the first part of a long paper may omit relevant evidence located in later sections. Evidence-aware classification is therefore an important practical problem for trustworthy LLM-assisted scientific review \citep{lin2026hallupeer}.

\paragraph{Challenge 1: Long, heterogeneous documents.}
Scientific PDFs include multi-column text, formulas, tables, and section-specific terminology. Extracting clean evidence spans is difficult, and a classifier cannot always process the full manuscript within its context budget.

\paragraph{Challenge 2: Relevance is not sufficiency.}
A retrieved passage may share terminology with a review claim but still lack the information needed to verify it. For example, locating the experimental section does not guarantee finding the exact numerical result mentioned in the review. Consequently, downstream classification F1 cannot by itself establish evidence-retrieval correctness.

\paragraph{Challenge 3: Fair comparisons and class imbalance.}
Changing the evidence source can also change the classifier's input distribution. Furthermore, minority hallucination categories can be masked by majority-class accuracy. We will use identical model weights, prompt structure, evaluation examples, and input budgets where feasible, while reporting macro F1 and class-specific scores.

\paragraph{Prior work and open question.}
Retrieval methods such as BM25 \citep{robertson2009probabilistic} and neural sentence embeddings \citep{reimers2019sentence} offer alternative ways to locate supporting passages. Retrieval-augmented language modeling demonstrates the general value of combining explicit documents with neural models \citep{lewis2020retrieval}. Recent research highlights that using information located at different positions in long contexts can be challenging \citep{liu2024lost}; the 2026 HalluPeer work presents a complementary paper-grounded benchmark for scientific-review hallucination detection and classification \citep{lin2026hallupeer}. Together, these studies motivate a controlled investigation of how evidence selection influences hallucination-type classification.

\paragraph{State of the art and scope.}
HalluPeer is a relevant task-level reference, but its published taxonomy contains nine coarse categories; we do not equate these with the five labels of our selected classification dataset \citep{lin2026hallupeer}. Our contribution is a controlled, reproducible analysis of evidence selection within one five-label benchmark. We will not compare numerical scores across incompatible label taxonomies.
